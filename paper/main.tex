\title{A GPU-Accelerated DREAMPlace/OpenROAD Evaluator Node for CHIA}
\subtitle{Timing-Driven GPU Placement, Cached Multi-Backend Evaluation, and Noise-Aware Agentic Search}

\author{Kaviprashanna Krishnamoorthy}
\affiliation{%
  \institution{Independent Researcher}
  \country{India}}

\begin{abstract}
Agents that tune physical-design flows need an evaluator that is fast,
accurate and reusable. A full place-and-route run of a large design takes a
long time, and its quality varies with the placement seed. We present a
reusable CHIA evaluator node built for this. One request/result contract drives
two flow backends: OpenROAD-flow-scripts (ORFS) on NanGate45 and LibreLane on
sky130. Placement runs on the GPU and routing on the CPU as separate CHIA
functions. Every result is cached by the content of its request, so replaying a
finished experiment runs no tools. The node places with DREAMPlace, made
timing-driven by reweighting critical nets from OpenSTA slack. On Ariane133 it
beats stock ORFS on timing (TNS $-323$ vs.\ $-530$\,ns, WNS $-0.226$ vs.\
$-0.322$\,ns), with 1.8$\times$ faster placement and comparable end-to-end
runtime. On three smaller designs TNS ties and routed wirelength is 2--5\%
shorter. On top of the node, five proposers, two of them LLMs, tune eight flow
knobs. A routed final round keeps the default unless a candidate beats it by
more than seed noise.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{physical design, placement, OpenROAD, DREAMPlace, LLM agents,
CHIA, design-space exploration}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Physical-design flows expose many parameters that trade off power,
performance and area (PPA), and recent work automates their tuning, from
black-box search to LLM agents. AutoTuner~\cite{autotuner} searches ORFS
parameters with derivative-free optimization, AutoDMP~\cite{autodmp} tunes
DREAMPlace macro placement with Bayesian optimization, and
ChatEDA~\cite{chateda} uses an LLM agent to plan and run RTL-to-GDS flows. Such
loops are slow and noisy. A place-and-route run of a large design takes a long
time, its quality changes with the placement seed, and a cheap estimate such
as the placer's own objective can rank candidates in the wrong order. An agent
that tunes the flow therefore needs an evaluator that is fast, returns accurate
results, and can be reused across designs, flows and proposers.
CHIA~\cite{chia} provides the agent loop; we contribute such an evaluator.

DREAMPlace~\cite{dreamplace} treats analytical global placement as
neural-network training. The cell positions are the trainable parameters. The
loss is a smooth wirelength model, whose smoothness is set by a parameter
$\gamma$, plus a density penalty that pushes local cell density toward a
target density. A gradient optimizer updates all positions at once with GPU
kernels. Placement stops when the overflow, the fraction of cell area above
the target density, falls below a threshold. OpenROAD~\cite{openroad} and its
flow scripts (ORFS~\cite{orfs}) form an open RTL-to-GDS flow that places with
RePlAce~\cite{replace}, and LibreLane~\cite{librelane} adds Magic/Netgen
signoff on sky130. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A reusable CHIA evaluator node: one request/result contract over two
    flow backends (ORFS on NanGate45, LibreLane on sky130), placement and
    routing as separate GPU and CPU CHIA functions, and content-addressed
    caching that replays finished experiments without rerunning tools
    (\S\ref{sec:node}).
  \item Timing-driven GPU placement inside ORFS: DREAMPlace interleaved with
    OpenROAD buffer repair and OpenSTA net weights. It beats stock ORFS on
    Ariane133 timing with placement 1.8$\times$ faster (\S\ref{sec:node},
    \S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A noise-aware search protocol (\S\ref{sec:protocol}). Candidates are
    scored after clock-tree synthesis, the cheapest stage whose timing
    predicts the routed ranking. A routed final round accepts a candidate only
    if it beats the default by more than seed noise.
  \item A comparison of five proposers (default, random, a tree-structured Parzen
    estimator (TPE)~\cite{tpe}, an
    LLM and an LLM with timing diagnosis) on four designs
    (\S\ref{sec:results}). On jpeg and tinyRocket the final round keeps the
    default. On swerv\_wrapper it adopts an LLM-proposed configuration that
    improves worst negative slack (WNS) on 11 of 12 test seeds. Its score gain
    is within seed noise.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Evaluator Node}
\label{sec:node}
\paragraph{Contract and backends.}
The node sits between a CHIA search and the flow tools
(Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). On the driver side, the experiment driver and the
search loop turn each proposer's configurations into requests. A
\texttt{CachedEvaluator} answers requests it has already seen and sends the
rest to CHIA functions on the workers. Placement runs as a GPU CHIA function
and routing as a CPU-only one, so routing never holds a GPU. An
\texttt{EvalRequest} names a design, technology, knob settings, seed, stage and
toolchain fingerprint. An \texttt{EvalResult} carries wirelength (WL), WNS and total
negative slack (TNS) per stage, power, area, the detailed-route design-rule-check (DRC) count
and provenance. The driver, search loop and proposers see only these two
records, so they run unchanged on either flow backend, chosen per experiment:
ORFS on NanGate45 or LibreLane on sky130. All experiments in this paper use
the ORFS backend. The LibreLane backend uses the same DREAMPlace handoff and
reaches Magic DRC\,=\,0 and Netgen layout-versus-schematic (LVS)\,=\,0 on \texttt{spm}.

\paragraph{GPU placement handoff.}
Stock (unmodified) ORFS runs until IO placement and writes the design as a
DEF file. DREAMPlace places it on
the GPU, and we read the cells back into OpenROAD. ORFS then continues
unmodified through resizing, legalization, clock-tree synthesis (CTS), global
and detailed routing and final reports. The final placement is DREAMPlace's,
snapped to legal sites. As a baseline, AutoDMP's best tinyRocket trial, run
through a format conversion outside its tested setup, routed with 159 DRC
violations.

\paragraph{Timing-driven GPU placement.}
Stock ORFS's global placer pauses twice, at overflow 0.63 and 0.20, to repair
the netlist and reweight critical nets. We do the same
around DREAMPlace.
DREAMPlace runs until the overflow falls to 0.6. OpenROAD then loads the
placement, runs \texttt{repair\_design} and then re-buffers every net, and computes
per-net slack with OpenSTA. The worst 10\% of nets get weights of up to 5 for
the next DREAMPlace run, which resumes from the repaired placement. This
repeats at overflow 0.2, and a last run finishes at 0.1. The repaired netlist
moves between stages as an OpenROAD database, so later stages start from the
repaired netlist instead of redoing the repairs. Before
the first repair, nets with more than 2000 loads are split by a $5\times5$
grid of buffers, so that \texttt{repair\_design} does not spend minutes on a
single net. On Ariane133 this splits the 20,636-load reset net with 19
buffers and cuts \texttt{repair\_design} from 480 to 84\,s. Target density is
0.45 (set on Ariane133 and used for all designs). We call the result the
\emph{GPU flow}.

\paragraph{Determinism and caching.}
On gcd, repeating a request gives bit-identical results, even with 2 or 8
threads. A request's identity is a hash of its fields,
including the image digest and DREAMPlace commit. A \texttt{CachedEvaluator}
replays known identities. Replaying two finished experiment databases resolved all 804 (gcd) and 201 (tinyRocket) identities
without a single new evaluation. The searches in this paper served 5, 2 and 6
results from cache on Ariane133, jpeg and tinyRocket.

\paragraph{Search space and proposers.}
The protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}) has two stages: a search scored after
CTS, then a routed final round. Each proposer runs the search
more than once, and each repeat is a \emph{search run}. The search space is eight
flow knobs:
target density, density weight, wirelength smoothing $\gamma$, learning rate,
the overflow values at which DREAMPlace pauses for repair, full or incremental
re-buffering, and the share of nets that get weights and the maximum weight. The objective puts timing first:
$\mathrm{score} = -\mathrm{TNS} + 100\cdot(-\mathrm{WNS})$, in ns. Lower is
better, and 10\,ps of WNS counts as much as 1\,ns of TNS. The random proposer samples uniformly (log-uniformly for density
weight and learning rate). The LLM proposer (Gemini 3.1 Pro preview) makes one
call per round, which returns configurations as JSON, with the knob table and
its scored history. The
diagnosis proposer also sees what each run showed (per-stage repair and congestion
data, the worst paths and high-fanout nets) and must state a hypothesis for
each configuration. On Ariane133 each search run gets two rounds of four
configurations on search seed 3, and each round's best is re-run on seeds 4
and 5.

\paragraph{Cost and fidelity.}
Routing every candidate is too slow, so search stops at an earlier stage. That
stage's numbers need not match the routed result; they only have to put
candidates in the same order.
Fig.~\ref{fig:results}a compares the stages on 13 competitive Ariane133 runs
(final TNS better than $-700$\,ns). For each stage it shows how well its TNS
ranks the routed TNS, and how many minutes it takes to reach. After placement the ranking is weak
(Spearman 0.45, after 5 minutes). After CTS it is strong (0.86, after 21
minutes), and global route is no better (0.74, after 46). Search stops at CTS,
about a quarter of the 81 minutes of a full route. The placer's own estimate
is not a safe stage for wirelength either: on 60 gcd configurations, its
half-perimeter wirelength ranks the routed wirelength with Spearman $-0.29$.

\paragraph{Noise and the routed final round.}
The flow is deterministic for a fixed seed, but routing amplifies small
placement differences between seeds. The protocol uses three disjoint sets of
seeds: search seeds (3--7 on Ariane133), selection seeds (8, 9) and test seeds
(101 and up). Test seeds are never used by the search or the final round. We define $\sigma$ as the standard
deviation of the default configuration's score over five search seeds. On
Ariane133, $\sigma$ is 17\% of the mean score. A proposer's result
counts only if, on the test seeds, it beats the default by more than twice the
standard deviation of the default's routed score across those seeds. CTS is only a filter: on
swerv\_wrapper, a configuration three times better than the default at CTS
routed worse. After search, the four distinct configurations with the best mean CTS
score over at least two search seeds become the \emph{finalists}. In practice
these are configurations promoted as the best of their round. The finalists and the
default are routed on the two selection seeds. A finalist
replaces the default only if it wins by more than
a threshold $\theta = 2\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$, with $n=2$ selection seeds. The winner
is then compared with the default on the test seeds.

\paragraph{Against stock ORFS\@.}
On Ariane133, the design with the most cells and macros, the GPU flow
beats stock on both TNS and WNS (Table~\ref{tab:stock}), at 2.9\% longer

\paragraph{Search and final round.}
Table~\ref{tab:final} shows its results. The round picks an
LLM-proposed configuration for swerv\_wrapper that ranked last of four at
CTS. Over 12 test
seeds it improves WNS by $0.033 \pm 0.013$\,ns, on 11 of them, and shortens
wirelength by $1.7\% \pm 0.4\%$, on all 12. Its score change in
Table~\ref{tab:final} is within noise, because the score is dominated by TNS,
which varies widely between seeds on this design. On jpeg and tinyRocket
the best finalist's lead is within the threshold, so the default is kept. Adopting
those finalists would have tied the default on jpeg and lost to it on
tinyRocket (Table~\ref{tab:final}, $\dagger$). On
Ariane133 the best finalist's lead clears the threshold, which gives the
\emph{Ariane133 winner}.

On jpeg and tinyRocket every proposer's search-stage lead (up to 25\%)
vanished on test seeds. On the two macro-heavy designs the LLM proposers
found the configurations the final round picked. The Ariane133 winner, found by
the diagnosis proposer, changes three knobs: it pauses for repair at overflow
0.6, 0.4 and 0.2 instead of 0.6 and 0.2, raises the maximum net weight to 8
and sets $\gamma = 2$. The Ariane133 winner improves TNS on 7 of 8 test
seeds, by $+94 \pm 115$\,ns on average. Its per-seed TNS change ranges from
$-190$\,ns to $+398$\,ns (Fig.~\ref{fig:results}c). With two or three search
runs per proposer, no proposer's lead clears this bound, so the comparison does
not rank the proposers.

\paragraph{Runtime.}
Both flows were timed one run at a time, on the same machine under the same
load. On Ariane133, placement takes 498\,s with the GPU flow vs.\ 887\,s for
stock, 1.8$\times$ faster. The GPU placement itself takes about 10\,s, and the
rest is OpenROAD repair and timing analysis. End to end the flows are on par:
the difference is within the run-to-run spread. On tinyRocket the GPU flow is
1.4$\times$ slower.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Only global placement runs on the GPU. Timing-driven placement still needs
OpenROAD repair and OpenSTA on the CPU, and these take nearly all of the
placement time. CTS and routing take most of the end-to-end runtime. As a
result, the GPU flow matches stock end to end and is slower on small designs.
The evidence covers four designs in one technology, NanGate45. Only one of
them, Ariane133, is large and macro-dense, and that is where the GPU flow
improves timing. Our per-net slack step requires a flat netlist, so
hierarchical designs are flattened, and stock ORFS still has better WNS on
swerv\_wrapper. With two or three search runs per proposer and 4--12 test
seeds per design, the study cannot rank the proposers. CTS was validated as
the search stage on Ariane133 only. The LibreLane backend was tested on
individual designs and not used for search.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a reusable CHIA evaluator node with one request/result contract
over two flow backends, placement and routing as separate GPU and CPU CHIA
functions, and content-addressed caching. Its timing-driven GPU placement beats stock ORFS on Ariane133 timing
with 1.8$\times$ faster placement. On three smaller designs it ties stock on
TNS with shorter wirelength. Its noise-aware protocol keeps the default when
search gains are within seed noise. On Ariane133 the adopted
configuration's gain is within noise on test seeds; on swerv\_wrapper the
routed round adopts an LLM-proposed configuration that ranked last at CTS and
improves WNS on 11 of 12 test seeds. Its score gain is within seed noise.
